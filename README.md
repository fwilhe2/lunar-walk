# Surface Walk

A first-person planetary surface simulator with seventeen bodies in it, from the sun outward — **Mercury, Venus, the Moon, Mars, Phobos, Deimos, Ceres, Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Enceladus, Titan, Iapetus, Triton, Pluto and Charon**. Three.js, no build step, nothing fetched at runtime: every texture in the scene is generated in the browser at load, and the surface itself is generated forever as you travel. Walk, drive a rover, or fly.

## Run

ES modules need HTTP, so `file://` won't work:

```sh
python3 -m http.server 8000
```

Then open http://localhost:8000 and click to lock the pointer. The "GENERATING" screen is the opening rings of terrain being built in workers — a few seconds of CPU work, not a download.

## Desktop builds

The desktop app wraps the same local files in Electron, so it does not need a network connection or a separate server at runtime. Install Node.js 20 or newer, then run:

```sh
npm install
make start
```

Build an installer for the current operating system with `make build`, or choose one platform with `make build-linux`, `make build-mac` or `make build-windows`. Outputs go in `dist/` (Linux AppImage, macOS DMG and Windows installer). macOS and Windows releases are unsigned by default, so those operating systems may show their usual first-run security prompt.

GitHub Actions builds all three platforms on their native runners for pushes to `main`, version tags (`v*`), pull requests and manual runs. Each run uploads the installers as downloadable workflow artifacts for 30 days.

Sun elevation is the interesting one. At 5° the craters are all rim and shadow, and every shadow is a black pool that reaches across the ground; at 60° an airless surface flattens into a grey wash and you can barely read the ground — which is exactly the problem Apollo crews had judging distance near lunar noon. Turn your back to the sun at any elevation and it happens again: the shadows all hide behind whatever casts them, and the ground washes out into a featureless glare around your own shadow. On Mars it does something else entirely: the whole sky dims and deepens with it, because the sky *is* the sunlit dust.

One kernel generates all of them. Everything that differs between them — gravity, radius, crater populations, the shape of the ground and the colour of the dirt — is a row in a table that is shared **verbatim** with the mesh workers, so a chunk built off-thread and a footstep tested on the main thread never disagree about which world they are on.

## The surface is unbounded

There is no map, on any of them. Craters are never stored — they are re-derived on demand from integer cell coordinates through a hash, in one to six size classes per body, each class on its own uniform grid sized so a query touches only the 3×3 neighbourhood. Any coordinate you ever visit resolves the same craters, so each surface is infinite yet permanent: drive 30 km out, come back, and your tracks end at the same crater you left.

- **One height function** (`terrainHeight`, shared verbatim between the main thread and the mesh workers) drives the visible mesh, walking collision, all four rover wheels, rock placement and footprints, so you always stand exactly on what you see.
- **Chunk streaming**: nested levels of terrain chunks (256 m at 1 m resolution near you, out to 16 km chunks at 256 m resolution) build in Web Workers and follow you. Mercury, the Moon, Mars, Io, Europa, Pluto and Charon stream ±40 km, Enceladus ±14 km, past which from the flight ceiling the ground has dropped three kilometres below the horizon; Phobos and Deimos stop at ±3.5 km, because on a body 22 km across everything past that is kilometres below the horizon and would be pure waste; Venus stops at ±10 km, for the opposite reason — the ground is still there, but the air is in front of it.
- **The horizon is real.** Chunks curve away by d²/2R at each body's true radius. On the Moon that puts the edge of the world below the horizon at ~2.4 km; on Phobos, with a radius of 11 km, the ground falls half a kilometre away from you inside 3.5 km and the horizon closes to a few hundred metres. **On Venus it curves the wrong way.** 92 bar of CO₂ bends a horizontal ray on a radius of about 1,100 km, six times tighter than the planet curves under it, so light follows the ground down and then some and the surface never drops out of sight — it climbs. The apparent radius works out at −1,330 km: the ground reaches your eye line two kilometres out and keeps rising, and you stand in the bottom of a shallow bowl. The Venera panoramas show exactly this.
- **Craters** follow real simple-crater morphometry (Pike 1977): a fresh bowl is a fifth of its diameter deep, under a rim standing 3.6% of the diameter above the plain, and its ejecta blanket thins as the inverse cube of range (McGetchin 1973), which is what makes a young crater's rim a sharp crest and not a mound. Age takes the bowl down toward a shallow dimple and rounds the crest away. Past each body's **simple-to-complex transition** — about 15 km on the Moon, 10 on Mercury, 11 on Venus, 4.5 on Europa, 11.5 on Pluto and 14.5 on Charon — a crater collapses as it forms: it comes out shallower for its width, with a flat floor, a central peak and walls slumped into terraces behind a scalloped rim. Depth, rim height and floor width follow Pike's lunar power laws, joined to the simple bowl exactly at the transition so the population has no step in it, and scaled to each body by its own transition (Robbins et al. 2021 for Pluto and Charon). And most craters are old: below a few hundred metres a mare surface sits at crater **equilibrium** — each new crater erases, on average, one that was there — with a cumulative density close to 0.08 D⁻² per square metre, and at equilibrium four in five small craters are subdued ghosts, a few per cent are crisp. The size classes are set to land on that curve from 1.6 m to a kilometre, and each class skews its ages to match. Fresh craters on the airless bodies also wear a **bright halo** of immature ejecta the solar wind has not yet darkened, and the freshest large ones throw rays.
- **Below the mesh**, down to the millimetre, relief is shading only — nothing you can trip on. Sub-metre craters are generated per pixel in the ground shader, each bending the surface normal, casting its own shadow in closed form (a bowl's highest point along any ray is its rim), and hiding the part of itself its near rim would hide from your eye. The last few centimetres are a generated regolith texture: tens of thousands of clods and grains, soft aggregates, angular half-buried pebbles and a scatter of micrometeorite pits, blended three ways across a hexagonal grid of random offsets so the 3 m tile never visibly repeats.

### What makes each one look like itself

- **Mercury** — the Moon's cratering under two and a third times its gravity, so ejecta lands closer: blankets are narrower, and secondaries fall in dense clusters and chains near their parent. Most of the ground is **intercrater plains**, old and rolling, giving way in patches to **smooth plains** — lava floods, flatter, brighter and redder than the rest, and wrinkled by ridges where they shrank as they cooled. Then the whole planet shrank: as its iron core cooled Mercury lost some seven kilometres of radius (Byrne et al. 2014), and the crust broke in thrust faults, the **lobate scarps** Mercury is known for — cliffs a kilometre or two high, facing one way, with a long gentle back behind them, running for hundreds of kilometres in arcs that bulge toward the lower side, and cutting straight through the craters they cross. And in some craters are **hollows**: shallow, flat-floored, rimless pits tens of metres to a kilometre across, bright and faintly blue, in clusters on floors, walls and peaks, where some volatile in the rock is still subliming away into space (Blewett et al. 2011) — among the youngest things on the planet. Over it all lie broad patches of dark, bluish low-reflectance material, rich in graphite dug up from depth. You land on intercrater plains seven kilometres west of the face of a scarp nearly a kilometre and a half high, which the opening view looks at, among craters full of hollows.
- **Venus** — the atmosphere writes this surface, not by eroding it but by standing in the way of everything that would otherwise hit it. 92 bar shields the ground so well that **there is no crater on Venus under about 1.5 km**: anything that would make one is torn up and stopped on the way in. So there is one crater class, it is enormous, it is almost never there, and when it is it is usually pristine — about 85% of Venus's craters are, since little has touched them since they formed — the real planet has ~1000 craters on 460 million km², one per 680 km square, because it resurfaced itself with lava around 500 Myr ago and started the count over. What you walk on instead is that lava: sheets a few metres thick ending in steep fronts, **wrinkle ridges** where the cooling plain buckled, and **fracture swarms** where it pulled apart. The old crust survives as **tessera** — the ridge-and-groove terrain Ovda Regio is made of, two fabrics crossing at 60°, the roughest ground on the planet, standing a kilometre above the flows drowning it. Up on its crests is the strangest thing here: Venus has one climate, 464 °C everywhere, but it lapses ~8 K/km, so above **4.75 km** the ground is cool enough for heavy metal sulphides to condense out of the air onto it as a bright coating. Every radar map of the highlands shows that line, ignoring the geology and following the contour. The site sits at 4.3 km, so the crests cross it.
- **Moon** — mare basalt saturated with craters at every scale, because four billion years of bombardment had nothing to erase any of it. A continental-scale mask decides where the mare gives way to ridged anorthosite massifs, so a long traverse crosses plains, then mountains, then plains. Once in every thousand-odd square kilometres there is a crater 5 to 30 km across — the landing site is on the outer ejecta of a fresh one, 26 km wide and nearly 3 km deep, whose rim stands on the north-north-eastern skyline; fly over it and it has a flat floor, a central peak and a terrace. Fresh large craters throw bright ejecta rays. Across the mare run **sinuous rilles**, channels a kilometre or two wide and a couple of hundred metres deep that meander for tens of kilometres from a source pit, where lava flowed long enough to melt its way down into the ground — Hadley Rille, beside Apollo 15, is one; there is one three kilometres north-east of the landing site. Its walls are strewn with blocks shed off the ledges near the rim, as Apollo 15 photographed Hadley's from the edge. The soil is not grey but a faint brown-grey — mare reflectance climbs steadily toward the red — at 7–10% albedo.
- **Mars** — wind does the work impacts do elsewhere. Craters under a few metres do not survive at all and the survivors are shallow, half filled with sand. Fresh ones end in a **rampart**: a distal ridge where the ejecta, fluidised by ground ice, flowed out as a sheet and stopped. Transverse **dunes** gather in the lows with a long stoss slope and a slip face at the angle of repose, their crests bending and forking where two trains of dunes meet out of step; transverse aeolian ridges — symmetric, sharp-crested over flat troughs, a fifteenth as tall as they are apart, forking and dying out along their length — cover the lows between them, and layered mesas terrace into ~9 m beds where wind has cut into the stratigraphy. Every crater trails a dark **wind streak** downwind of itself, where sand has scoured the bright dust off its lee side — the most obvious thing about Mars from orbit.
- **Phobos** — saturated at every scale, and cut by the **grooves**: parallel troughs a couple of hundred metres wide, running in families at three bearings and breaking into chains of pits along their length. They are spaced a few hundred metres apart, as the real ones are, which matters more here than it sounds: any wider and they would be further apart than Phobos's own horizon, and you could walk around the moon without ever finding one.
- **Deimos** — the same rock with a thicker blanket on it. Metres of regolith drape and infill every crater, so it reads visibly smoother, and it has no grooves. Two thirds of its surface is under 5° of slope; on Phobos it is one fifth.
- **Ceres** — the largest body in the asteroid belt, a dwarf planet of rock, clays, salts and ice under a dark carbonaceous regolith. Its craters have straight-sided, polygonal rims, guided by fractures in the crust (Otto et al. 2016), and the largest are fewer than a body this old should keep, relaxed away by the ice in its crust. You land inside **Occator**, 92 km across and four deep, about twenty million years old and the brightest place on Ceres. Its floor is lobate material that flowed and froze, cut by fractures; its rim stands all round the horizon forty kilometres off, its foot already below the curve of a world 940 km across. You stand on the lip of the central pit, 9 km wide and 800 m deep, with **Cerealia Tholus** in it, a fractured dome 3 km wide and 400 m high — and over the pit and the dome lies **Cerealia Facula**, sodium carbonate five times as reflective as the rest of Ceres, left where brine rose from a reservoir below and its water boiled off into vacuum (De Sanctis et al. 2016, 2020; Nathues et al. 2020). The thinner Vinalia Faculae lie in patches on the floor to the east. At a sun this low the pit's near wall is in shadow, and its floor shows only when the sun climbs (`]`).
- **Io** — the most volcanic body in the solar system, heated from inside by the tide Jupiter raises in it, and resurfaced so fast — about a centimetre a year — that **not one impact crater** has ever been seen on it. What you walk on is plains of sulphur and frozen sulphur dioxide, fallen from plumes and condensed from the thin air, flat for tens of kilometres and layered, the layers ending in low scarps where the frost that holds them sapped out (Moore et al. 2001). Out of the plains sink **paterae**, the volcanic craters: flat-floored, steep-walled pits kilometres to tens of kilometres across and hundreds of metres deep, floored with black lava, and rimless, because they are collapses, not cones; each wears a halo of what its vent throws out, red short-chain sulphur or white SO₂ frost. And out of them rise **mountains**, which are not volcanoes: blocks of crust thrust up and tilted where the crust, loaded by the lava piling on it, fails in compression (Schenk & Bulmer 1998) — one long edge a scarp, the other a ramp, landslide aprons at the foot. Dark lava flows run out in long lobes. Colour is sulphur in its forms: cream and yellow, white, orange-red, black. You land on the plains near Kanehekili Fluctus, in the red fallout ring of a patera 15 km across, with a mountain 7 km high on the east-north-eastern skyline and Jupiter standing over it.
- **Europa** — ice, and almost nothing on it made by an impact: the surface is 40–90 million years old, young enough that craters are rare, and most of the small ones are secondaries, which arrive in clusters and are absent everywhere else. What covers it is tectonics, from a shell flexed twice a day by Jupiter's tide. **Ridged plains**: generation after generation of ridges, each grown along a crack that cut through everything older, so every younger ridge buries the ones its flanks rest on — completely if it is the bigger, a notch if not — and which generation dominates changes from place to place. On top of them the **double ridges**, two crests and a central trough, a hundred to three hundred metres high and one or two kilometres across, the youngest wearing the dark flanking margins of a triple band. **Bands**, strips of grooved new ice kilometres wide where the shell pulled apart. **Chaos**, where it nearly melted through: the plains broken into rafts that drifted and turned in a hummocky matrix and froze again, each still carrying its ridges, now out of line with its neighbours — Conamara's jigsaw. The rafts are the cells of a Voronoi diagram, evaluated over the plains as they were before they moved, so they tile the ground along straight fractures and open apart toward the chaos interior. And **lenticulae**, domes and pits and dark spots a few kilometres across, most with a small chaos on top. The ice is nearly white; what darkens it is non-ice material cooked by Jupiter's radiation — reddish-brown along the lineae, and in the chaos the yellow-brown of irradiated sea salt that Hubble found in **Tara Regio**, which is where you land, on the edge of a chaos, with the rafts between you and Jupiter. It lies in the lows as a lag; the crests keep the frost; steep faces shed it and show clean ice, and shed blocks, which lie on them as talus.
- **Ganymede** — the largest moon in the solar system, bigger than Mercury, with two kinds of ground. A third of it is the old **dark terrain**: cratered nearly as densely as Callisto, dark lag in the lows and frost on the crests and poleward slopes, a few knobs, and **furrows** — troughs kilometres wide with raised rims running for hundreds of kilometres in great arcs, the oldest tectonics on the moon. The rest was torn open and resurfaced as **bright terrain**, in **sulci**: bands of cleaner ice tens of kilometres wide, set a few hundred metres below the dark terrain they cut through, and grooved along their length — ridges and troughs three to ten kilometres apart with a few hundred metres of relief, often tilted fault blocks, sawtooth in section, with finer grooves a kilometre apart on them (Pappalardo et al. 1998, 2004). The grooves come in sets, each its own domain with its own trend, cut off sharply where the next begins. On the bright terrain the dark material has slid off the crests and walls into the troughs, so the ridges read bright and the troughs grey. Bright terrain is younger and carries a few times fewer craters; the oldest crater classes here stand only on the dark terrain. You land in a sulcus in Nicholson Regio, on a groove crest three kilometres in from its western margin, with the grooves marching away east under Jupiter. Ganymede is the only moon with its own magnetic field, which shelters its low latitudes from Jupiter's radiation; the readout keeps the dose, but here it is an upper bound.
- **Callisto** — the oldest surface in the solar system, and the most heavily cratered: saturated with craters at every size from tens of kilometres up. But not below that. Callisto's ice sublimes where the sun warms it, the dark non-ice part stays behind as a lag and slumps into the lows, and over four billion years that has erased the small craters — under a kilometre there are ten times fewer than on the Moon and fewer still the smaller you look (Moore et al. 1999) — and broken their rims down into **knobs**: steep, isolated hills tens of metres to a hundred high and a few hundred across, crowding along the rims of the old craters they came from, their crests frosted bright where the ice has frozen out again. Between them the plain is a smooth dark blanket, smoother at the metre scale than lunar soil. Frost also lies on every slope that faces the pole, which is colder; fresh craters punch through to clean ice and throw bright rays. Past a few kilometres the craters are complex, and past thirty they have a **central pit** where a rocky body's would have a peak (Schenk 1993). You land on the outer rings of **Valhalla**, the largest multi-ring impact structure in the solar system — a bright plain 600 km across ringed out to nearly 2,000 km from its centre — where the rings are graben: troughs a few kilometres wide and a few hundred metres deep between inward-facing fault scarps, running north–south past the site in broken segments, the nearest three kilometres east under Jupiter. This is where a crewed mission to Jupiter would land: Callisto orbits outside the radiation belts that make Io and Europa lethal (Troutman et al. 2003).
- **Enceladus** — a 500 km moon with an ocean under its south pole, and the ocean coming out. Across the south polar terrain run the **tiger stripes**, four parallel fractures 35 km apart, each a trough about 500 m deep and 2 km wide between twin ridges a hundred-odd metres high; along them water vapour and ice grains jet out at hundreds of metres a second, most falling back as snow, the rest feeding Saturn's E ring (Porco et al. 2006). Between them is funiscular terrain, ropey ridges a kilometre apart and a few tens of metres high, scattered with blocks of ice tens of metres across. Everything is under the fallout, which makes it the brightest ground in the solar system — a geometric albedo of 1.4 — and almost free of craters; the stripes' own ice is coarser and fresher, faintly blue. The stripes are drawn in lanes like Charon's graben. You land 5 km from the axis of Baghdad Sulcus, on the side toward Saturn.
- **Titan** — the only other world with liquid standing on it: seas and lakes of methane and ethane round the north pole, under 1.5 bar of nitrogen at 94 K. You land at the head of a bay on the southern shore of **Ligeia Mare**, with a plateau across the water, 400 km across and up to 160 m deep, so clear that Cassini's radar saw its floor (Mastrogiuseppe et al. 2014). Its coast is drowned river valleys — canyons cut down into the plateau country that the sea has since come up — and the land behind is a low coastal plain, then plateaus behind scarps a couple of hundred metres high, pocked with **sharp-edged depressions**, steep-walled and raised-rimmed: full of liquid where their floors lie below the sea's level, dry where they don't, and then floored with the brightest ground on Titan, the evaporites the liquid left (Hayes 2016). Craters are almost absent: the air stops small impactors and rain and sediment bury the rest. The ground is dark organic sediment, the haze's fallout moved by liquid, with **rounded water-ice cobbles** in patches on channel floors and along the shore, as Huygens saw where it landed.

  The sky is orange from horizon to zenith, the sun only a brighter part of it, and the light is a thousandth of Earth's daylight, almost all of it from the sky at once, so nothing casts a shadow you could see; Saturn stands overhead and never moves, and is lost in the haze. The cold, dense air bends light a fifth of the way back toward the ground, so the horizon sits further off than the planet's curve alone would put it. **The sea is a mirror**: Cassini found it smooth to a millimetre most of the time (Zebker et al. 2014), so it reflects the haze and the far shore — drawn each frame from the eye mirrored in the surface — with only faint ripples, and it is dark beneath, 1.5% reflective straight down and nearly total at grazing (n = 1.28). Near the shore you see the floor through it. Walk in: a suited body is denser than liquid methane, so you sink, but the liquid holds up 70% of your weight, and you walk the bottom at a third of Titan's gravity, slowed by the drag of a liquid, in a brown murk that fades everything past twenty metres.
- **Iapetus** — two surprises. Along its equator runs a **ridge**, a wall of mountains up to 13 km high — higher than anything on Earth — over more than a third of the way round the moon, broken into segments and isolated massifs and as cratered as everything else, so it is old (Porco et al. 2005; Giese et al. 2008); nobody is sure what raised it, a spun-down bulge frozen in or a ring that fell. And half of Iapetus is **black**. Its leading side sweeps up dark dust spiralling in from Phoebe's ring; dark ground warms, its ice sublimes and freezes out again on colder ground nearby, which brightens that and darkens the warm ground further — a runaway that has sorted the surface into lag at a reflectance of 0.04 and frost at 0.5, with almost nothing between (Spencer & Denk 2010). You land near the edge of the dark Cassini Regio, where both show in a mottle at every scale: dark plains, and frost on every slope that faces the pole. The ridge's crest is 22 km south, six kilometres up; the face you see looks north, and is white. Everything is saturated with craters, and in this gravity the transition to complex ones is out at 15 km.
- **Triton** — Neptune's captured moon, going round it backwards, with one of the youngest surfaces in the solar system: tens of millions of years old by its craters, which are few and fresh (Schenk & Zahnle 2007). You land where two terrains meet. To the north is the **cantaloupe terrain** of Bubembe Regio, the strangest landscape Voyager 2 found: dimples 25–35 km across, each a shallow dish a few hundred metres deep ringed by ridges, packed edge to edge like the rind of the melon and knobbly all over — most likely diapirs, blobs of warmer ice that rose through the crust (Schenk & Jackson 1993) — crossed by double ridges like Europa's. To the south, three kilometres off, begins the **polar cap**, Uhlanga Regio: nitrogen ice laid over the ground, smoother, brighter and faintly pink-cream from organics made in it by sunlight, streaked with dark fans tens of kilometres long that all point north-east. Those are dust from the **geysers**. Sunlight passes into the clear nitrogen ice, warms the dark material under it, and the gas breaks out carrying dust: Voyager caught four erupting, each a dark column a kilometre or so across rising straight up 8 km into the thin air, where the wind takes it level for more than a hundred kilometres (Soderblom et al. 1990). Two stand on the cap to the south and south-east, their trails running away west — the wind aloft blowing the other way to the wind that laid the streaks — and sinking toward the horizon with distance, as the curve of a small world takes them. At 38 K this is the coldest surface ever measured.
- **Pluto** — on the side that faces Charon, which New Horizons saw only on approach, at tens of kilometres a pixel: the dark belt that runs most of the way round the equator, the same old ground as Cthulhu Macula. The crust is water ice, as hard at 40 K as granite, and everything is under a mantle of **tholins** — organic dust made from methane by sunlight in the haze and settling out of it for four billion years — which softens everything below the kilometre scale, so the plains roll. Craters are few for the age, and fewer the smaller they are: the Kuiper belt is short of small bodies (Singer et al. 2019), so there is almost nothing under a few hundred metres. Out of the mantle stand ranges of ice crust a few kilometres high, and on their summits is the strangest thing on Pluto: **methane frost capping them like snow**, but for the opposite reason to snow on an Earth mountain — Pluto's air is warmer higher up, and richer in methane, so above a certain height methane condenses onto the ice rather than off it (Bertrand et al. 2020). It follows height, not geology, with a ragged lower edge. You land on flat ground nine kilometres west of a range, with Charon over it.
- **Charon** — on **Vulcan Planitia**, the smooth plains that cover the southern half of the hemisphere New Horizons saw close up. When the ocean under Charon's crust froze it swelled, split the crust along the belt of chasmata to the north, and something — a slurry of water and ammonia, most likely — welled up and flooded the south in plains a kilometre or two below the old ground (Beyer et al. 2019). So it is flat, far flatter than anything else on Charon, and rolls only gently over tens of kilometres. It is cut by **graben**: long, flat-floored troughs a few hundred metres wide and up to a couple of hundred deep, where the plains pulled apart as they cooled, drawn in lanes like Europa's ridges so a lane boundary can never seam. And out of it stand the strangest landforms on Charon, **mountains in moats**: blocks of the older crust a few kilometres high, each ringed by a trench a kilometre deep, as if the flood had lapped against them and stopped short — Kubrick Mons is 3 km high in a moat 1 km deep. Only three are known on the whole plain, so they are rare here too, about one to every fifty thousand square kilometres. Craters are old and sparse, and short of small ones: Charon is where the Kuiper belt's shortage of small bodies was first measured (Singer et al. 2019). The ground is grey water ice at a reflectance of about 0.4, with brighter ice on steep walls, bright rays around fresh craters, and here and there a crater that dug into ammonia-rich ice and threw out dark ejecta instead, like Organa. You land on level plain 16 km west-south-west of a 4 km massif, 15°S and 45° west of the point beneath Pluto, with Pluto standing over the massif.

## The light

- On Mercury the sun is 0.39 AU off: a disc 1.4° across, two and a half times the Moon's, delivering 9.1 kW/m² — 6.7 times the Moon's light, enough to hold the ground at noon over 400 °C. It is kept in units 6.7 times the Moon's, so the numbers read like the Moon's and only the stars, which do not care, are relatively that much fainter. The ground is a little brighter than lunar mare, about 12%, so shadows are black but fill a shade more from the bounce around them. There is no night lamp: nothing overhead lights a Mercurian night, so the sun stays up. The Hapke set follows MESSENGER's photometry (Domingue et al. 2016): close to the Moon's, a little brighter and a little less rough.
- On Venus there is **no sun in the sky and no shadow on the ground**. Twenty kilometres of sulphuric acid cloud and 92 bar of gas leave about 17 W/m² on the surface — one part in eighty of what the Moon gets — and every photon of it arrives scattered from the whole sky at once. Nobody standing on Venus has ever seen where the sun is, so neither do you; there is only a vague brightening over half the sky. The ground is ordinary grey basalt, and everything orange about it is the light.
- Ceres is 2.77 AU out: 177 W/m², an eighth of the Moon's, from a sun 0.19° across, kept in units ten times the Moon's so its numbers sit near the Moon's. The ground is as dark as lunar mare, so the light is the Moon's: black sky, black shadows filled only from the ground round them — and the faculae, five times brighter than anything else, stop the eye down when they fill the view.
- Io shares Europa's sun and Europa's units. Its ground is nearly as bright — sulphur and frost at a reflectance of about 0.6 — and yellow, so the shadows fill warm, and it is tone mapped with ACES, as Mars is, because AgX gives away exactly that yellow in the highlights. Its nanobar of SO₂ does nothing to the light. Standing over the horizon are **plumes**: a Prometheus-type umbrella 90 km high 170 km to the north, and 700 km to the west a giant like Pele's, 300 km high, its lower hundred kilometres behind the curve of the planet. Nothing slows the spray on an airless world, so every grain flies a clean parabola and they crowd into a thin paraboloid shell — the envelope of all their paths — which is drawn in closed form along the line of sight, so it is brightest at its limbs, with filaments fanning through it and a column up the middle. The grains are small and scatter forward: the plume toward the sun glows, the one across it is faint, and a plume's top stays sunlit a while after sunset below it. The radiation is the worst anywhere you can stand: about 36 Sv a day, a lethal dose in three hours.
- On Europa the sun is five times further off than at the Moon: a disc a tenth of a degree across delivering 50 W/m², one part in twenty-seven. That is still an overcast day's worth of light, falling on ice ten times as reflective as lunar soil, so the eye stops down, and the key it adapts to sits higher than on the dark bodies — an eye anchors white on the brightest surface in view, and ice should read as white, not as the mid-grey the Moon's mare does. The same reflectivity lights the shadows: with no sky to fill them, they are lit by the sunlit ice around them, which returns two-thirds of what it gets, so they are several times brighter against the lit ground than a lunar shadow is — and they are the colour of the ice. Earth's snow shadows are blue because the sky lights them; these are not. Jupitershine adds under 1%.
- Ganymede shares Europa's sun and units too, on ground halfway between Callisto's and Europa's: dark terrain about 0.3, the grooved ice about 0.5, so a sulcus is visibly paler than the old ground at its margin.
- Callisto shares Europa's sun and Europa's units, on ground a third as reflective — a dark lag at about 0.2 — so the shadows fill less from the ground around them, and the eye opens wider. The frost is three times as bright as the lag, so the knob tops and the poleward slopes stand out white against it, as they do in every Galileo picture.
- Enceladus is 9.5 AU out: 15 W/m², a ninetieth of the Moon's, from a sun 3.4 arcminutes across, kept in Europa's units. It falls on snow, so the eye stops right down and the snow reads white, and the shadows fill from it more than anywhere else. The **jets** stand in a curtain along the stripe to the north: some hundred jets a kilometre or so apart, each spreading as it rises, merging into a sheet hundreds of kilometres high (Spitale et al. 2015) — drawn as a ribbon on the stripe's own trough, followed from the kernel, each jet a Gaussian widening with height, brighter where you look along the sheet. Its grains are a few microns across and scatter strongly forward, so it blazes toward the sun and is faint with the sun behind you; after sunset the moon's shadow climbs it from below, and the tops stay lit. Walking here is a 0.2 m/s shuffle and a full push keeps you up for half a minute, so the suit carries the Martian moons' jets, with a beacon to recharge them.
- Pluto is 33 AU out: 1.26 W/m², a thousandth of the Moon's sunlight, from a sun 58 arcseconds across — **a point, not a disc**, though still some two hundred and fifty full moons bright. Noon there is lit like the first minutes after sunset at home, and at that level, against a black sky, **the brighter stars stay out at noon** with the ground in view. Ten microbars of nitrogen do nothing to the light but hold up a haze — twenty-odd layers of it, to 200 km, the blue ring in New Horizons' departure picture — which from the ground is a faint blue glow low down, strongest toward the sun where it scatters forward, and a trace of blue fill in the shadows; the zenith is all but black. It is modelled as single scattering from a haze of vertical optical depth about 0.005, at the faint end of what is measured. The ground mixes dark tholin and bright frost, so its Hapke set is a compromise and the vertex colours say which is which.
- Iapetus is at Saturn's 9.5 AU — 15 W/m² — kept in units a hundred times the Moon's (Titan's), since in Europa's its black lag would sit two orders of magnitude under the Moon's radiance, where the eye's exposure amplifies the output dither into speckle. The lag is darker than any lunar soil and the frost ten times brighter, so the eye meters on whatever fills the view, and a glance from the plain up at the ridge stops it down.
- Triton is 30 AU out — 1.5 W/m², a point of a sun — kept in Pluto's units. The ground is nitrogen frost that sends back three-quarters of what falls on it, so the shadows fill from the ground round them, but the brighter stars still stay out by day, as on Pluto. Fourteen microbars of nitrogen hold up a haze a shade thinner than Pluto's, a faint glow low down. The geysers' dust is dark and fine: it dims what lies behind it, Neptune included, and scatters a little sunlight forward, so a trail toward the sun shows grey against black and one across it barely at all.
- Charon has the same sun, in the same units, and no air at all: no haze, no glow, a black sky. What changes is the ground, grey ice four times as reflective as Pluto's tholin, so the eye stops down and only the brightest of the stars that stay out on Pluto survive a view with the ground in it. That reflectance also fills the shadows. With no sky, the fill is sunlight bounced off the ice around them, grey and several times fuller than Pluto's brown. Plutoshine adds a few parts in a thousand of the sun. The Hapke set is Charon's own, fitted to New Horizons and Hubble images together down to a two-hundredth of a degree of phase: bright grains (w = 0.70), a phase function more isotropic than other icy moons', a narrow opposition surge, and 28° of roughness.
- On the other airless bodies: one hard sun, near-black shadows, a black sky at noon. What reaches into a shadow is light bounced off whatever sunlit ground can see into it — the lit far wall of a crater, the plain around a boulder — so it rises and falls with how much ground the sun is lighting. Earthshine is four thousand times fainter than sunlight and adds nothing you could see; on Phobos, **marsshine**, a 42°-wide disc of reflected sunlight overhead worth about 2% of the direct beam, is the only other thing in a shadow. Deimos, seven times further out, gets a seventh of that.

### Night

On the bodies with something hanging overhead, `[` takes the sun below the horizon, and the night is lit by that: **full Earth from the Moon**, Saturn from Enceladus, 3%, a four-thousandth of the sun and still, to a dark-adapted eye, the light of a well-lit street; Mars from Phobos, 2% of the sun and rust-red; Jupiter from Io, 1.5%, and from Europa, 0.6%; Charon from Pluto, 0.04%; Pluto from Charon, 0.2%. Each is geometric albedo times the square of the sine of the disc's angular radius, times its phase — and the phase comes round to full as the sun goes down, because a primary seen from its locked moon is gibbous at dusk and full at midnight. None of them moves, so the ground's shadows are marched toward it once at sunset, the same way as the sun's; and each is a disc, not a point, so its shadows are soft — Earth's by two degrees, and Mars from Phobos, 42° across, barely casts one at all. Turn your back on Earth and your own shadow stretches out ahead of you across ground lit the grey-blue of earthlight.

The eye adapts to all of it, and the stars come out with the ground still in view — a few bright ones and a faint Milky Way under a full Earth, a sky full of them on Charon, whose nights are thirty times darker. The primary itself, in sunlight you are no longer in, is a dazzling disc; look straight at it and the eye stops down until the clouds are back.

### Shadows, at every range

The ground shadows itself — every crater and hill, from a pit at your boots to a massif ten kilometres off. It can be done cheaply because the sun here only ever moves in elevation, never in azimuth, so for any point on the surface the question of whether it is lit reduces to one number: how high the skyline stands in the sun's direction. The streamed terrain is rendered top-down into four nested height clipmaps (half a metre a texel near you, 32 m at 16 km), and a GPU pass marches every texel toward the sun and stores that skyline. Each pixel of ground then makes a single texture fetch and compares the skyline against the sun's **true angular radius**, so penumbrae come out sharp at a crater's rim and softening with distance from it, as a 0.53° sun makes them. Rocks, the rover, footprints and dust take the same test, seen from their height above the ground; moving the sun costs nothing at all. The maps are rebuilt only when the ground under them changes.

Below that, shadow maps in two cascades handle what stands on the ground. The near one is ±24 m at 2.3 cm a texel with about a centimetre of depth bias, because the sun's penumbra behind a boulder is a centimetre wide and a rock's shadow has to start exactly where the rock meets the regolith. Your own body casts one too, and nothing else of it is drawn: turn your back to the sun and your shadow stretches out ahead of you, helmet and backpack and all, walking when you walk.

### Regolith is not Lambertian

Nothing about the Moon looks right while its soil is treated as matte paint. It is a fluffy, porous layer of dark grains, and it reflects according to **Hapke's model**, implemented in full in the ground shader with the published lunar parameters (Sato et al. 2014, from LROC):

- a **Lommel–Seeliger** core, which makes a lit surface equally bright from every viewing angle — the reason the full Moon has no limb darkening, and the reason the ground brightens toward the horizon;
- a strongly **backscattering** single-particle phase function, so the same ground is five times brighter looking down-sun as looking across the sun at a low sun, and dark looking into it with every rim picked out in light;
- an **opposition surge** from shadow-hiding over the first few degrees and coherent backscatter inside the first half-degree — the halo around the photographer's shadow in every Apollo surface photograph, which here rings the shadow of your helmet;
- **macroscopic roughness**, ~22°, which fills the surface with shadows too small to resolve and darkens it toward grazing angles.

Each body gets its own set: Phobos has the strongest surge in the set (the darkest, most porous regolith anyone has measured), Mars a weak one because skylight fills the gaps between grains, Venus none, because a surge needs a collimated source, and Europa the brightest grains — a single-scattering albedo of 0.96 (Domingue et al. 1991) — with a surge only a fraction of a degree wide, and grain-scale shadows half as deep, because light scatters into them through the ice. Boot prints are compacted soil, which has lost the porous structure the surge comes from, so they almost vanish looking across the sun and turn dark looking down-sun — as they do in the Apollo pictures.

- **It sparkles** — on the airless bodies. Sparse micro-facets of impact glass catch the sun within a dozen metres of your boots, and only where the sun reaches; on Europa the facets are frost. Mars and Venus have none: that glass is made by micrometeorite melting, and both of them have air.
- Albedo tracks slope, elevation, province and age, and the sign flips between bodies. On the Moon scoured slopes and young ejecta expose brighter material; on Mars they are *darker*, because what slopes shed there is the bright dust, leaving dark basalt.

### Eyes, not a camera

Under a low sun that ground spans a range no fixed exposure holds — five times brighter down-sun than across it, fifty times darker in a crater's shadow than beside it — so the view **adapts** the way an eye does, quickly toward the light and slowly toward the dark. It meters the brightest *surface* in view rather than the average pixel, so half a frame of black sky does not overexpose the ground in the other half. From the sunlit surface the sky is simply black, as it is in every Apollo photograph; look up with no lit ground in view, or stand in a shadow, and over a few seconds the eye opens and the brightest stars come out. The sun is an HDR disc at its true angular size — 0.53° from the Moon, 0.35° from Mars and its moons, where it also delivers 43% of the flux — with the glare an eye gives a point source: a steep scattering core and a fringe of faint radial streaks, and no aureole, because there is no air to make one. Then a mild visor grade: vignette, grain, a little colour split at the edges. The airless bodies tone-map through AgX, which keeps a shadow at 2% of the lit ground a very dark grey you can see into; Mars and Venus through ACES, which keeps the colour in a sky that has one.

## The sky

- The **Milky Way** crosses the sky at a slant: a generated band with patchy star clouds, dark dust lanes, and a warm bulge.
- **9,000 stars** with a power-law magnitude distribution and blackbody colours — dim ones skew red, beacons skew blue — rendered as hard round points, because there is no air to twinkle. Both are drawn at brightnesses the dark-adapted eye can reach and the daylight-adapted one cannot, so they appear only when you look away from everything the sun is lighting.
- On Venus the sky is **inverted from every other body here**: the zenith is the bright part, because that is the short way out, and the horizon is dark, because that way the column never ends. Rayleigh scattering over that much gas is tens of optical depths deep in the blue and thin in the red, so what survives to the ground is orange. The same scattering is what limits you to a few kilometres of visibility, and it is why distance here reads as reddening rather than as fading.
- On Mars, both are switched off and a dust-scattering dome switched on. Six millibars of CO₂ scatters next to nothing by itself; what you see is the micron ferric aerosol it carries, at an optical depth around 0.5 on a clear sol. That inverts Earth's sky in every respect: dust scatters long wavelengths, so the bulk of it is butterscotch — but the particles are large compared to the wavelength, so their forward-scattering lobe is narrow and comparatively neutral and piles up in a halo a few degrees around the sun, which therefore reads **blue** against an orange sky. Nobody predicted it; Viking 1 photographed it in 1976. The same dust hazes the distance, so the fog takes the sky's own colour and both dim together as the sun goes down.

## What hangs overhead

Every companion body is stated at an angular size, so they are comparable to each other, and most sit on the same 6,200 m shell; the Galilean moons are drawn just inside or outside it, so that one crossing in front of Jupiter is in front of it. The fixed ones are placed anti-sunward, because a body on the sun's side of the sky shows you its night.

- **Venus and Earth from Mercury** — both near opposition, full and as near as they come: Venus 50 arcseconds across and Earth 29, so both are points, drawn a little over life size only so they survive being rasterised, like Deimos from Mars. Their brightness per unit of disc is the physical one — sunlight at 0.72 and 1 AU, a third and a seventh of Mercury's, on albedos of 0.75 and 0.3 — which makes Venus the brighter star. Earth is where the rover's dish points.
- **Earth from the Moon** — 1.9° across, drawn at 4.8° on purpose. Coastlines start as coarse lon/lat polygons and are resampled through a fractal displacement field, which turns straight edges into ragged, self-similar coasts, then coloured per-pixel: ice caps, boreal forest, the two desert belts at ±25°, a narrow continental shelf. The clouds come from the general circulation — a convective band at the ITCZ, the dry subtropical highs either side of it, the stormy midlatitudes with their comma-shaped cyclones, the Southern Ocean under an almost unbroken deck — about two thirds of the planet covered, which is what makes Earth from space mostly white and blue. A custom shader does the rest: a soft terminator, specular glint off ocean and only off ocean, city lights on the night side dimmed under cloud, and a Rayleigh veil over the whole day side — the reason the oceans look blue and not black — thickening to a glowing rim at the limb.
- **Mars from Phobos** — 42° of sky, drawn at true size because it needs no help. At that size the map is magnified far past anything a canvas could hold, so it is built in three layers, each covering a scale the one before cannot:
  - **albedo** — the classic map, Syrtis Major and Acidalia and Mare Erythraeum and the rest, painted over the global ferric dust that gets redistributed every dust season, plus Valles Marineris, the Tharsis shields with their dark calderas, both polar caps, and a steep power law of craters that survive on the ancient southern highlands and are mostly buried under the young northern plains;
  - **elevation** — what the albedo cannot carry: the crustal dichotomy, the Tharsis bulge with Olympus standing off it, Hellas and Argyre and Isidis. It is what lets the terminator rake across real topography instead of a painted ball;
  - **procedural detail** — generated per fragment in the shader from 3D noise on the sphere, so it stays sharp however close you get, and bends the normal along with the baked relief.

  Phobos is tidally locked, so Mars hangs in one place and never rises or sets — but it turns underneath, one Martian day against the 7h39m Phobos takes to go round. From Deimos the same view is 17°. The atmospheric shell is 1.2% of the radius, not Earth's 3.5%: Mars's scale height barely stands off its limb.
- **Phobos and Deimos from Mars** — 0.20° and 2 arcminutes. Phobos is drawn at 2.5×, the same exaggeration as Earth; Deimos is left as what it is, a bright star. Phobos crosses the sky *westward* in about four hours and Deimos keeps the real ratio to it, creeping. By day you see only their lit part. The dust that makes the sky lies between you and them as well, so their night side is exactly the colour of the sky beside it — as the dark part of the Moon in a daytime sky at home is blue — and the light they do send is dimmed on its way down through that dust.

- **Jupiter from Europa** — 12.2°, at true size, which is twenty-four times our Moon, and flattened to 0.935 at the poles by its own spin. The map is built the way the planet is: a latitude profile of pale zones and brown belts, displaced by turbulence stretched along the jets and stirred by eddies on every boundary, then the named features — the Great Red Spot in its hollow, oval BA, the String of Pearls, the brown barges of the North Equatorial Belt, and along its southern edge the blue-grey hot spots trailing festoons across the Equatorial Zone. True colour: cream and tan and brown, and only the Red Spot is red. Its limb darkens less than a matte ball's (Minnaert), it has a thin shell of haze, and its brightness is the physical one — sunlight on an albedo like the ice underfoot — so it hangs about as bright as the ground. It never moves. But Europa's orbit, Jupiter's equator and the orbits of the other Galilean moons all lie in one plane, which from Europa's surface is a great circle through Jupiter, and from Tara Regio — 10°S, 75° west of the point beneath Jupiter — that circle climbs almost straight up out of the eastern horizon. So **the bands stand nearly on end**, the Red Spot rides down the disc rather than across it as Jupiter turns, and only from near the line through Europa's poles and the sub-Jovian point would the bands lie level.
- **Jupiter from Ganymede** — 7.64° across, fifteen full Moons, 34° over the eastern horizon from Nicholson Regio (15°S, 55° west of the point beneath it). Io and Europa orbit inside Ganymede and cross Jupiter's face; Callisto, outside, goes behind it. Night under it is 0.23% of the sun.
- **Jupiter from Callisto** — 4.35° across, eight full Moons, 14° over the eastern horizon from Valhalla (15°N, 75° west of the point beneath it), and fixed there. Io, Europa and Ganymede all orbit inside Callisto, so all three cross Jupiter's face from here, and pass behind it. Night under it is 0.08% of the sun.
- **Saturn from Enceladus** — 29° across, fifty-eight full Moons, at true size, 24° up in the north-east, flattened to 0.90 by its spin, computed from the site (55°S, 45° west of the point beneath it). Its bands are Jupiter's kind, muted under a deep haze to butterscotch and cream, with the winter hemisphere's high latitudes gone azure and the hexagon round the north pole. Enceladus orbits in the ring plane, so **the rings are edge-on**: a line through the planet and far out either side. They are drawn properly all the same — an annulus with the measured radial structure, C ring, B ring, Cassini Division, A ring with the Encke Gap, the F ring, lit as a slab of particles by single scattering, the bright B ring on the sunlit face and the thin rings glowing on the other, Saturn's shadow across them — and the shadow they throw onto the globe is not edge-on at all: each point on the cloud tops follows the sunlight back to the ring plane and is dimmed by the optical depth it crosses there, so a dark band lies across the planet, widening as the sun climbs away from the ring plane. Tethys, Dione, Rhea and Titan wander along the ring line and Mimas crosses the disc. Night under Saturn is 3% of the sun.
- **Jupiter from Io** — 19.5° across, thirty-nine full Moons, at true size, 40° above the east-north-east horizon, computed from the site (18°S, 48° west of the point beneath it) the same way as from Europa; Europa, Ganymede and Callisto circle outside Io's orbit, so none ever crosses Jupiter's face from here, but each goes behind it, Europa as much as 0.72° across. Night under it is 1.5% of the sun.
- **Io, Ganymede and Callisto from Europa** — at their true sizes and distances as they move, all along that same arc. Each is placed where the geometry of the two orbits puts it: Io swings out to 39° from Jupiter and back, grows to 0.84° — bigger than our Moon — as it passes in front of the disc, and shrinks to 0.19° behind it; Ganymede and Callisto, outside Europa's orbit, go right round the sky. Io is sulphur, black paterae and a red plume ring; Ganymede dark and bright terrain with frost caps; Callisto dark and spattered with bright craters.

- **Saturn from Iapetus** — 1.9° across, the rings 4.4° tip to tip, 40° up in the east (1.7°N, 50° west of the point beneath it). Iapetus is the one big moon whose orbit is tilted well out of Saturn's equator, so from here, unlike from every other, the rings are seen **open** — by 12°. From near the equator the ring plane is the great circle through the east point and the zenith, so they stand nearly upright. Titan wanders past as a small orange disc.
- **Neptune from Triton** — 8.0° across, sixteen full Moons, 29° above the eastern horizon from the site (15°S, 60° west of the point beneath it), and fixed there. In true colour, as Irwin et al. (2024) recalibrated Voyager's pictures: not the deep azure of the famous stretched images but a pale greenish blue, a shade bluer than Uranus, with faint banding, a dark belt round the south pole, the Great Dark Spot with white cirrus along its southern edge, the Scooter and the second dark spot, and long white streaks drawn out along the latitudes by the fastest winds in the solar system. Triton's orbit is tilted 157° to Neptune's equator, so the planet's bands are not along its moon's orbit. Night under it is 0.2% of the sun.
- **Charon from Pluto** — 3.65° across, seven Moons, at true size, and it never moves: Pluto and Charon are locked face to face, so it hangs in one place over one hemisphere and is never seen at all from the other, where the famous heart is. Its position is computed from the site — 5°S, 60° west of the point beneath it, 27° above the eastern horizon — including the three degrees of parallax from standing on the surface of a body only sixteen radii away. Its north pole, and Pluto's, points along the horizon, so **Mordor Macula**, the red-brown stain on Charon's pole made from methane that escaped Pluto and froze out there, sits on its left-hand limb, and the canyon belt runs up and down the disc. It is grey water ice at an albedo of 0.4 in the same sunlight as ground at 0.1, so it is the brightest thing in the sky after the sun; its light in a shadow is four hundredths of a percent of the sun's.
- **Pluto from Charon** — 7.1° across, fourteen full Moons side by side, at true size, 42° above the east-north-east horizon, computed from the site the same way including a degree and a half of parallax. It never moves either. The face it keeps toward Charon is the one Pluto's own site is on: the dark equatorial belt, here a string of separate red-brown maculae west of Cthulhu, the long dark whale that runs a third of the way round, under pale ice that is cream toward the equator, banded across the northern mid-latitudes and greyer over the polar cap. The heart is on the far side and never turns this way. Its north points along the horizon, so the cap is on the left-hand limb and the belt runs up and down the disc. Brightness is physical, calibrated the same way as Charon's: the map holds albedos, 0.08 in the maculae to 0.9 on the brightest ice. The haze is a thin blue line on the limb, faint from this side of the sun because it scatters forward. Both globes are turned on their poles so the hemisphere facing the other body is the one you see.

From Venus, nothing. The cloud deck is opaque in both directions: no Earth, no sun, no stars, and a rover down there talks to an orbiter it cannot see, so its dish sits at the zenith and waits for the pass.

Every phase follows the sun, so `[` and `]` change all of them.

## Rocks

Rocks stream with the ground and cluster where ejecta lands — ringed thick around young craters, all but gone around old ones, which have ground their blocks down to soil. They are fractured blocks rather than lumps: each shape is a noise-roughened ellipsoid cut by a handful of random planes, which is how rock breaks, with normals creased so the fracture faces meet at real edges. Three levels of detail, several shapes each, pebble to boulder. Each rests on a low face with a small tilt and is sunk by its true lowest point below the lowest ground under it, so no edge floats and no shadow comes loose from its rock. Fresh rock is a little brighter than the mature soil around it, dust settles on whatever faces up, and up close the surface is pitted and vesicular.

## The lander

On the Moon the lunar module you came down in stands 24 m east of the landing site, under Earth in the opening view: built from primitives to the real thing's dimensions, like the rover. The descent stage is an octagon 4.2 m across in crinkled gold Kapton, black on its upper deck, with the engine bell under it; four legs on 0.94 m footpads spread 9.4 m, three with their contact probes bent over on the ground; the porch, and a ladder that stops a long step short of the forward pad. On it is the ascent stage — still there, since you have not left yet — with its two triangular windows over the hatch, the aft equipment bay, the docking tunnel, four RCS quads and the dishes, seven metres up in all. Each pad stands on the ground where it is and the struts take up the difference, as the crushable real ones did. It is solid: you stop against it, and so does the rover.

Around it the ground is a little brighter. LROC sees every Apollo descent stage in a halo of soil some 10% more reflective, a hundred-odd metres across, where the engine blew the fluffy top layer of the regolith away in the last seconds of the descent.

Elsewhere something else stands near the site, placed in Node on the levellest ground in the opening view and with a clear line of sight from where you arrive, each pad on its own ground and the body levelled on the plane fitted through them. On Mars it is a **Viking** lander, as in Chryse and Utopia in 1976: a hexagonal bus 1.5 m across on three legs, RTG wind covers on its flanks, the stereo camera turrets, the high-gain dish on its mast, the weather boom, and the sampler arm reaching out to its trench. On Venus it is **Venera 13**, which worked for 127 minutes at 457 °C: a titanium sphere on a crushable landing ring, under the disc of the drag plate that was all the air brake it needed in air that dense, its helical antenna on top, its colour chart and penetrometer arm on the ring. Everywhere nobody has landed yet it is what you came down in, a crewed lander the size of the LM, in white paint and silver foil. Phobos and Deimos keep only the beacon.

## The rover

The Lunar Roving Vehicle, built from primitives to its real dimensions: a tubular aluminium chassis on double wishbones; wheels with a spun-aluminium hub and a tyre of woven zinc-coated piano wire, shaped like any other tyre, with titanium chevrons over half the tread; pale fibreglass fenders; two webbing seats with the console and T-handle between them; the LCRU up front under white thermal blankets, with the umbrella high-gain dish, the helical low-gain antenna and the TV camera on their masts; the tool pallet at the back. While you drive, the commander sits in the left seat with a hand on the T-handle. The dish's weave is cut out, so light — and shadow — passes through it. The tyre's mesh is too fine to survive that at any distance, so it is painted, and opens only when you are close enough to draw its holes cleanly — then the bump stop shows through. The high-gain dish stays pointed at the relay while you drive. Bicycle-model steering with no authority at rest; each wheel follows the terrain on its own pair of wishbones, which swing about torsion bars under the chassis and keep the wheel upright; chevron wheel tracks stamped into the regolith, and rooster tails of dust off every wheel at speed, thrown from where the tread leaves the ground.

It drives on forces. Four 0.25 hp hub motors push 480 kg — rover, you and your kit — up to the 3.6 m/s their gearing allows, and only downhill any faster, which is how Apollo 17 logged 18 km/h. Every force the tyres make driving or braking is capped by their grip, about 0.4 of the load on lunar soil: nine seconds to full speed on the Moon, most of a minute on Charon. Sideways they hold better than they pull, since a wheel sunk into the soil has to bulldoze regolith to slide, but a turn tighter than even that just plows wide. Rolling through soil costs a tenth of the weight. The parking brake holds wherever grip allows, and a rover you bailed out of mid-jump still comes down.

It has mass. The chassis is a rigid body on four sprung, damped wheels with bump stops, with its own inertia in pitch and roll, and the springs are scaled to the body's gravity so it rides at the same height everywhere — what changes is how slowly it all happens. At a sixth of a g the suspension swings slow and deep, and a rover coming over a crater rim at speed keeps rising after the ground has stopped: it flies, steering nothing, pitches over in the air, and comes down on its bump stops in a ring of dust, as the Apollo 16 Grand Prix footage shows. Grip follows the actual wheel loads, so it squats when it pulls away, dives under the brake, and leans out of a turn against its anti-roll bars.

It is available on Mercury, the Moon, Mars, Ceres, Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Titan, Iapetus, Triton, Pluto, Charon and Venus, though on Venus it crawls: dragging a rover-sized frontal area through 65 kg/m³ at walking pace takes about a kilowatt, so it tops out at 1.7 m/s and does not coast — it stops. On Phobos, Deimos and Enceladus pressing `R` tells you why not: a wheel needs weight on it to make traction, and at six thousandths of a g there is none to be had — spin a wheel there and you lift the rover, not the regolith.

## Physics

Gravity is each body's real value, and nothing about moving on foot is tuned for feel. You are an 80 kg person in 91 kg of suit and backpack — the Apollo 16 journal says of John Young that "the suit and backpack weigh as much as he does" — and everything follows from that mass, the local gravity, the grip of the ground and what a pair of legs can do.

- **The push.** Leg force falls off linearly with extension speed, the force–velocity profile of jumping (Samozino et al. 2008): 36 N per kilogram of body at rest, nothing left at 4 m/s. Unsuited on Earth that is a 0.33 m squat jump. The suit's joints take 42% of the force and its knees allow only a 0.25 m crouch, which calibrates a maximal lunar jump to **0.82 m, Charlie Duke's on Apollo 16**, just before he fell over backwards; John Young's casual jump salute was 0.42 m. You choose the effort: hold `Space` to sink into the crouch — the readout shows the effort and the takeoff speed it will give — and let go to push. Hold a direction and the push tilts that way, within the friction cone, which is how you jump forward. A push weaker than your weight only stands you back up.
- **The pace.** Legs are pendulums, so natural speeds scale with the Froude number v²/gL. Suited Apollo crews changed from walking to running at Fr 0.36 (Carr & McGee 2009), and J-mission crews ran for long stretches at 1.4 m/s, Fr 1.35. The same two numbers set your walk and your run (`Shift`) everywhere.
- **The grip.** Every horizontal force comes from friction, so nothing speeds you up, slows you down or holds you on a slope harder than μ·g·cosθ — about 1 m/s² on the Moon. On ground steeper than the friction angle, around 33°, you slide. Ice at these temperatures has no film of melt on it and grips nearly as well as soil.
- **The gait.** Steps follow Alexander's dynamic similarity, a stride of 2.3 L·Fr^0.3, shortened by the suit to what pose estimation of Apollo video measured: 0.39 m steps at 0.42 m/s, both feet down 40% of the time, 0.36 m apart. A walk vaults the eye over each planted foot and sways it over each in turn, about 2 cm either way on the Moon. Past Fr 0.5 you lope as the crews did, a skip of trailing foot, leading foot, flight: contact lasts as long as on Earth, so in low gravity the flight is what grows — 0.7 s and 10 cm up at a lunar run, two seconds on Charon. Prints, footfalls and dust come at the strikes. The gait moves the eye, not the physics: averaged over a stride a bounce pushes on the ground with exactly your weight.
- **The lean.** To speed up or slow down on legs you have to lean until the ground's push passes through your centre of mass, tan φ = a/g. The Moon allows a sixth of Earth's friction under a sixth of its gravity, so the lean is just as steep — up to 30°, the posture in every Apollo film of a crewman getting going — but it comes and goes at √(g/L), far more slowly. With full head motion (`V`) the view takes half of it (your head takes back the rest inside the helmet); your shadow always takes all of it, on top of the 16° the backpack makes you stand at.
- **The helmet.** Your head turns only so far inside it. Look down past about 25° and you bend at the hips to see, look up and you lean back, and the eye swings about the hips as slowly as the suit lets it — which is how the crews looked at the ground at their feet, and how they had to lean right back to photograph Earth overhead.
- **The effort.** The readout keeps your metabolic rate and heart rate, and your breathing in the helmet follows them. Standing in a pressurised suit costs about 140 W. Each step adds work against the suit's joints, which gravity does not change, and a share of your weight carried along, which it does; climbing is paid at muscle's 25%. On the Moon that is about 300 W walking and 450 W loping, the span of the Apollo crews' telemetry (Waligora & Horrigan 1975). On Mars or Venus the same Froude pace is a hard run under 171 kg, and your heart says so. Past about 1 kW, what a fit person can hold for an hour, you draw on a reserve of some 80 kJ (the critical-power model); run it dry and you are winded, and walk until you have a third of it back — on Mars after nine minutes at a run, on Venus after half of one, on the Moon never.
- **Your body.** The shadow you cast is a whole suited figure, pinned to your eye, so the shadow of your helmet sits exactly on the antisolar point. Part of it is drawn too: bend right over to look at the ground and your legs and overshoes, with their blue soles, come into the bottom of the view. The suit's own bulk keeps your chest out of sight, as it did for the crews.
- **The rocks.** Every stone from 15 cm up is solid. One you can lift a boot onto — the suit's knees allow about 0.3 m — is ground: you step up onto it, the eye easing up over a stride, and step down off it. Anything taller stops you where it rises past that height, with the grip of glove and boot on rock, so a push square on stops you rather than steering you round it.
- **Falling.** Every crew that went far on foot fell. Here you fall only from things that would do it: running square into a boulder (it knocks you back), catching a toe on a stone mid-lope, landing a jump too fast on a downslope, and now and then a full-effort jump straight up, which turned Charlie Duke over backwards when his backpack's weight came round. You topple at the pace your height and gravity allow, slow and oddly gentle on the Moon, lie there a moment, then push yourself back up, which shows on your heart rate.
- **The power.** Once moving, legs deliver a few hundred watts, which is what limits climbing, and what sets your pace on Venus.
- **In the air you are a projectile.** Nothing you do changes where you land, and you leave the ground by yourself wherever it curves away faster than gravity can follow, where v²κ > g. On a light body at a run, that is every hump. Landing sinks you into the knees as far as the legs need to stop you.

**In the suit on Earth you cannot leave the ground at all.** 171 kg weighs 1,678 N, and the legs, through the suit, make 1,670.

**On Phobos and Deimos you cannot walk.** Grip there is three and two millimetres per second squared: starting, stopping or turning on foot takes minutes. A full push is not a jump but a launch — ten minutes in the air on Phobos — so you push with a fraction of the effort, and move with the suit's jets. They are the Manned Maneuvering Unit's, as flown from the shuttle in 1984: 0.09 m/s² along each axis, and 36 m/s of Δv in a full nitrogen charge, shown in the readout. They only ever add velocity, so stopping takes as much gas as starting, and when the tank is empty you coast. The beacon at the landing site recharges them. Deimos lets go of you at 5.6 m/s, and the tank holds six times that.

**On Pluto you weigh a sixteenth of what you do at home** — an 80 kg astronaut weighs what a 5 kg bag does — and under half of what you would on the Moon. A full push clears two and a half metres and keeps you in the air for six seconds, and with a third of the Moon's grip, starting, stopping and turning all take a long time.

**On Charon you weigh three hundredths of what you do at home**, which is less than half again of Pluto: an 80 kg astronaut weighs what a 2.3 kg bag does. A full push puts you nearly six metres up for twelve seconds, but walking is a shuffle at 0.3 m/s, and a run a lope at 0.6. It is still nowhere near a launch: escape velocity is 590 m/s.

**On Enceladus you weigh about a hundredth of what you do at home**, an 80 kg astronaut what a kilogram bag does. A full push puts you fourteen metres up for half a minute, and walking is a shuffle at 0.2 m/s with grip to match, so the suit carries the same jets as on Phobos and Deimos, and the beacon recharges them; they are stronger here, against a gravity barely more than their thrust. Escape velocity is 240 m/s, out of reach of legs or jets either.

**On Europa the physics is easy and the radiation is not.** The ground is ice at −170 °C, as hard as rock and not slippery — ice is slippery on Earth because it is near its melting point — and the gravity a little under the Moon's, so you lope. But Europa orbits inside Jupiter's radiation belts, and the commonly quoted surface dose is 5.4 Sv a day, against 1.4 mSv on the Moon and 0.64 on Mars; four to five sieverts at once kills half the people who receive it. So there the readout keeps count of your dose from the moment you arrive. Tara Regio is on the leading hemisphere, which is spared some of the electrons the trailing one takes, so here that figure is if anything an overestimate.

**On Venus gravity is not the problem.** At 0.904 g your weight is nearly what it is at home; what stops you is that 92 bar of CO₂ at 464 °C is not air but a fluid at 65 kg/m³, a twentieth the density of water. Walking there is wading. Pushing a suited walker through it at 2 m/s costs about 130 watts, and the drag climbs as the cube of speed, so the ceiling on a run is power rather than traction: at 400 W it stalls at 2.9 m/s. The drag is quadratic and three-dimensional, so it shortens a jump as well as a stride, and Archimedes gets a say too — that much fluid holds up about 7% of your weight.

Landing throws dust. In vacuum it flies in clean parabolas and drops, with no billowing, because there is no air to suspend it. Every grain in a plume has its own size and albedo — a haze of fines, most of them smaller than a pixel and drawn only as faint as the share of a pixel they cover, with clods in it — and each is lit as a small rough sphere — shaded across its face when it is big enough to show one, at its phase angle when it is not — and shadowed by the ground and by the rover, rocks and walker it flies past. The fines also scatter forward, as sixty-micron grains do — the same physics as the lunar horizon glow — so a plume thrown up at a low sun shines against the ground looking down-sun, lights up again looking into the sun while its clods hang dark in it, and goes dark where it passes through a shadow. Mars is one exception: six millibars is not much, but it is not nothing, so fine grains feel drag and the plume lags, spreads and hangs, while the clods drop out of it. Venus is the other, four orders of magnitude further along — a 50 µm grain of basalt settles through that fluid at about 20 cm/s, so kicking the soil there throws nothing anywhere. It stands up in a slow cloud around your boots and takes the best part of a minute to come back down. Boot prints and wheel tracks stay where you put them, lie on the ground's own grain rather than over it, and press the pebbles under them into the soil.

Flight mode is the one deliberate fiction on seven of these bodies — there is no lunar aircraft to model — and the one place it is not is Venus, where a wing needs a seventh of the speed it does on Earth and the Soviet VEGA balloons flew for two days without trying. It is still capped per body, as height above the ground beneath you rather than an absolute altitude, so the streamed horizon always reaches past what you can see wherever you are.

## Performance

Three quality tiers, guessed from the GPU's name the first time — Intel and other integrated graphics start on **low** — remembered after that, and cycled with `Q`. The `RENDER` line in the readout shows the frame rate, the internal resolution and the tier.

The tiers trade what costs the most on a weak GPU and leave what makes it look like the Moon: Hapke photometry, terrain shadows at every range, the eye's adaptation and the shadow a rock casts are the same on all three. What low gives up is the pixel ratio above 1, MSAA (FXAA instead), bloom, soft shadow filtering, anisotropic filtering beyond 2×, the three-way blending that hides the regolith tile's repeat, a third of the terrain's vertices beyond the nearest 400 m, two thirds of the pebbles and their shadows, and half the resolution of the terrain-shadow maps. Medium and low read Hapke from a precomputed table rather than evaluating it per pixel.

On top of the tier, the internal resolution floats: it is measured once a second and lowered until the frame rate reaches the tier's target — 26 fps on low — then raised again when there is room. It goes by the typical frame rather than the average, so the odd stall while new ground streams in does not make it hunt.

## Sound

There is none from outside, on six of the eight — nothing carries it. What you hear is what reaches you through the suit: your own breathing in the helmet, quickening as you work and slowing back over half a minute; the backpack's fans and pump; your boots striking the ground, carried up through your bones as a dead thud with the grit of the soil in it; the jets hissing through the backpack frame; the rover's hub motors and its springs bottoming out, through the seat. Apollo crews heard their rover only that way. When something comes over the loop, the Quindar tones key and unkey it, 2525 and 2475 Hz.

On Mars six millibars of CO₂ does carry sound, badly — about 20 dB quieter than at home, the highs absorbed within metres — so the grit under your boots and the rover's motors reach you through the air too, faintly, and the wind is a low rumble at the edge of hearing. On Venus the air is a twentieth as dense as water, sound carries loud and far, and a breeze of a metre a second, pushing 32 pascals against you where a Martian gale pushes one, is a roar. Everything is synthesised; nothing is fetched. `M` turns it off.

## Finding your way

There is no map, and on an unbounded surface the one fixed point is where you landed. A heading tape runs along the top of the view — north is −z, as the position readout counts it — with the flag (or the beacon) on it, the sun, and whatever hangs overhead; when the flag is behind you, its mark waits at the edge you would turn toward. The readout gives its distance and bearing. Apollo 14 walked to within a few tens of metres of Cone crater's rim and turned back without knowing: on the Moon the horizon is two and a half kilometres away, and everything on it looks the same distance off.

Because every surface is a pure function of where you stand, where you stand is enough to share a view: the address bar always holds the world, the place, the heading and the sun (`#w=moon&x=…`), and `L` copies it. Anyone who opens the link stands where you stood, under the same sun. Shared positions stop at 100 km from the landing site, where the 32-bit vertex coordinates start to quantise at a centimetre.

## Demo mode

`0`, or the link on the opening screen, and it plays itself: a clean background with no crosshair and no readouts.

It holds no privileges. It presses the same keys and turns the same head a player does, and everything downstream of that — traction, jump arcs, suit jets, the rover's steering, chunk streaming — runs exactly as it would under a pair of hands, so what you are watching is the simulation rather than a scripted flythrough. It works in acts: walk somewhere, stop and look at whatever is overhead, take the rover out, go up for the vista, and every few minutes move on to the next body. On the moonlets it hovers on the jets a couple of metres up instead of walking, because that is what you would do. The sun creeps the whole time, since on an airless world the shadows are the scenery.

Any key, or a click, hands the controls back.
